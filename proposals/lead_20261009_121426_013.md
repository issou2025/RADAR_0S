## Summary

Keep the nautical theme. Before 1.0, while renames are still cheap, fix the few names whose cost outweighs what they carry. The two worth acting on are the **pier / quay** distinction and the **"signal"** collision with the `signals` package. Everything else should stay.

## Why the theme is worth keeping

The concepts harbor handles don't have names in Flutter. Flutter can say "safe area" and "padding". It has no words for:

- a bar the content runs under, as against a bar the content stops at;
- a sheet that pushes the page up rather than covering it;
- the space left once the system insets, your own bars and the keyboard are all counted.

Plain descriptive names for these come out long, collide with words Flutter already uses (inset, padding, safe area), or don't hold together as a set. The nautical set does hold together, and its relationships come for free: the tide (the keyboard) rises over the coast (the system insets), docks sit at the edges, and clear water is what's left. A coherent metaphor is easier to reason with than unrelated descriptive names, for people and for code assistants.

Renaming would not remove the learning cost either. The concepts themselves are new. Calling a breakwater a `PushingSheet` still leaves a reader to learn what it does. The concept map added in #30 already gives each term its nearest Flutter equivalent, and the `FlutterError` hints from #27 carry the rest at the point of misuse.

## Where the theme costs more than it returns

### 1. Pier vs quay: the distinction depends on nautical knowledge

`HarborDock.pier` (content runs under it, as with `extendBodyBehindAppBar`) and `HarborDock.quay` (content stops at it, as with `Scaffold.appBar`) is a real distinction, and the most important one a dock makes. But few readers know which word means which. The meaning sits in the trivia rather than in the metaphor. That the README needed a note on how "quay" is pronounced (#37) points the same way. The tide stances (`HarborTideStance.float`, `.pilings`, and the dry dock) have the same problem to a smaller degree.

Options, in order of preference:

- **Keep one dock and name the behaviour as a parameter in plain words, inside the theme.** For example, one dock kind with a parameter that says whether the body runs under it or stops at it. The tide stance could be named the same way: rides the keyboard, stays put, or keeps its space. The metaphor stays, and the part a reader must get right is spelled out.
- **Keep `pier` and `quay`, and make the difference unmissable**: put the behaviour into each constructor's first dartdoc line and the analyzer hover text, not only the concept map.

The first option is a breaking rename. Doing it before 1.0 is far cheaper than after.

### 2. "Signal" collides with a popular state-management package

`signals` is a widely used Flutter state-management package. To many Flutter developers, and to code assistants trained on that ecosystem, `HarborSignals` reads as reactive state rather than as a toast. A nautical word that is still free would avoid this: a flare, or a horn. It stays in the theme and means "a brief message that gets attention".

"Anchor" is a much smaller concern. `MenuAnchor` uses it to mean nearly the same thing, so the overlap helps rather than hurts.

## What I would leave alone

- **Coast, tide, clear water, fairway, moored:** intuitive enough on first read, and the concept map covers the rest.
- **Buoy, beacon, lighthouse:** they land well; a beacon that keeps something in sight is exactly the image.
- **Breakwater:** a genuinely good name for something Flutter has no word for.

## A note on adoption

An app that adopts harbor behind its own small adapters (its own padding, scroll-view and page-host widgets) keeps harbor's vocabulary inside that layer, and its feature code keeps speaking in Flutter's terms. That is where a themed vocabulary belongs anyway. It makes the two fixes above matter most for the adapter authors and for anyone reading harbor's own docs and errors. Those are exactly the people who meet pier, quay and signal first.
